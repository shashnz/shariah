2Main Discussion

Sources, framework, and features of Shariah compliance

Shariah compliance rests on a hierarchy of legal sources, is enforced through a formal governance structure inside every Islamic bank, and expresses itself through concrete differences from conventional banking. (rewrite balik supaya selaras dengan style penulisan kita)

2.1 Sources of Shariah

Primary Sources

(rewrite balik ayat) The ultimate foundation of Islamic law. Every banking concept must align with these two sources.

Al-Qur'an

(rewrite)The primary divine text providing absolute principles on commerce, prohibiting riba, and promoting fair trading.

As-Sunnah

(rewrite) The traditions and sayings of Prophet Muhammad (PBUH) that explain and clarify practical business dealings.

Secondary Sources

(rewrite) Supplementary sources used by scholars to derive rulings for modern financial issues.

Ijma' (consensus)

(rewrite) Unanimous agreement among qualified Shariah scholars on a specific legal issue.

Qiyas (analogy)

(rewrite) Applying an existing divine ruling to a new situation based on shared underlying reasons ('illah).

Istihsan (juristic preference)

(rewrite) Choosing a more suitable solution to avoid hardship or unfairness in transactions.

'Urf (custom)

(rewrite) Recognized local business practices that do not contradict any Islamic principles.

Istishab (presumption of continuity)

(rewrite) Presumption that a past legal state continues to exist until proven otherwise (e.g., contracts remain valid unless proven void).

Maslahah al-Mursalah (public interest)

(rewrite) Public interest decisions that bring benefit or prevent harm to society where no specific text exists.

Sadd al-Zara'i (blocking the means)

(rewrite) Blocking lawful actions that could inevitably lead to an unlawful or prohibited outcome (e.g., preventing hidden riba).

Qaul as-Sahabi (companions' fatwa)

(rewrite) The legal opinions or rulings given by the Companions of Prophet Muhammad (PBUH) on Islamic jurisprudence.

•Regulatory Timeline (rewrite semua, detail dan lengkap)

Evolution of Shariah governance in Malaysia

Malaysia's Shariah compliance framework did not appear overnight — it was built up over four decades of regulation, each layer closing gaps found in the one before it.

1983

Islamic Banking Act (IBA)

Malaysia's first dedicated legislation for Islamic banking, enabling Bank Islam Malaysia Berhad — the country's first full-fledged Islamic bank.

1997

Shariah Advisory Council (SAC) Established

BNM forms the SAC as the highest Shariah authority for Islamic finance, later given binding legal status under the Central Bank of Malaysia Act 2009.

2010

Shariah Governance Framework (SGF)

BNM introduces a formal governance structure requiring every Islamic financial institution to have its own Shariah Committee and internal Shariah review/audit functions.

2013

Islamic Financial Services Act (IFSA)

Consolidates Islamic banking and takaful regulation, giving SAC rulings explicit legal force over Malaysian courts and arbitrators.

2019

Shariah Governance Policy Document (SGPD)

Supersedes the 2010 SGF, strengthening board oversight, Shariah Committee independence, and a bank-wide "Shariah compliance culture" — effective 1 April 2020.

Section 3 • Critical Analysis

Where Shariah Compliance is Being Tested Today

( rewrite balik) Evaluating digital fintech pressure, legal form versus ethical substance, and pricing benchmark dependencies in contemporary Islamic banking.

Issue 01 ⚡️

Digital & Fintech Pressure

Onboarding, e-wallets, robo-advisory, and crowdfunding platforms move faster than traditional Shariah approval cycles. Ensuring real-time compliance in automated digital contracts remains a primary operational test.

Issue 02 ⚖️

Shariah-Compliant vs Based

Scholars distinguish products meeting basic legal form ("compliant") from those built on true risk-sharing principles ("based"). Heavy reliance on Tawarruq creates ongoing debate regarding practical authenticity.

Issue 03 📈

Benchmark & Pricing Dependency

Benchmarking Islamic financing profit rates against conventional interest rates (BFR/KLIBOR) challenges public perception, driving the urgent call for an independent Islamic economic pricing model.

Section 4 • Real-Life Application

Islamic Financial Contracts in Practice

How core Shariah principles are structured into everyday modern banking products.

01. Profit-Sharing Partnership

Mudharabah Investment

A trustee profit-sharing contract where the capital provider (Rabb-ul-Mal) provides 100% of the capital, while the entrepreneur (Mudarib) manages the venture. Profits are shared according to a pre-agreed ratio.

📌 Applied in: Investment accounts, term deposits, and wealth management.

02. Cost-Plus Sale

Murabahah Financing

A cost-plus profit sale contract where the bank purchases an asset requested by the customer and sells it back at a cost plus a disclosed profit margin, payable via deferred installments.

📌 Applied in: Home financing, auto financing, and asset acquisitions.

03. Commodity Murabahah

Tawarruq Monetization

A tripartite structure involves buying a commodity on deferred terms and selling it immediately to a third party for cash. It satisfies immediate liquidity needs while strictly adhering to Shariah rules.

📌 Applied in: Personal financing, Islamic credit cards, and liquidity management.

5Reflection

What this topic teaches about ethics and professionalism

Working on this topic changed how I look at something I used to take for granted — that a bank labelled "Islamic" is automatically doing things the right way. Going through the sources of Shariah, the governance structure, and especially the real case on non-compliant income disclosure, I realised Shariah compliance isn't a one-time checkbox. It's a routine — proposals get reviewed, audited, and sometimes a bank still slips up. What matters is whether they catch it and fix it properly, not whether they're perfect.

The hadith we opened with, Umar bin Al-Khattab telling traders not to sell in the market without understanding the rules first, stuck with me the most. It's basically the oldest version of what a Shariah Committee does today — making sure the person handling money actually understands what makes it halal or haram before they touch it. That single idea ties the whole topic together: knowledge comes before permission to act, whether you're a 7th-century trader in Madinah or a product manager launching a savings account in 2026.

In terms of ethics and professionalism, this topic showed me that "compliance" in Islamic finance isn't just a legal or regulatory word — it's closer to accountability with a moral weight attached. A conventional bank that breaks a rule pays a fine. An Islamic bank that lets riba slip into a product isn't just breaking a regulation; it's compromising the entire reason the product was allowed to exist. That's a heavier kind of responsibility, and it's probably why the Shariah-compliant vs Shariah-based debate matters so much to practitioners — compliance on paper isn't the same as living up to the spirit of the rule.

Looking forward, I think this space is only going to get more complicated, not less. Fintech, digital onboarding, and things like crypto and Islamic crowdfunding are moving faster than Shariah review processes were ever designed for. Malaysia is already close to 46% Islamic financing share of the whole banking system, heading toward the government's 50% parity target — so this isn't a niche corner of finance anymore, it's becoming half the industry. That makes getting the governance right, not just the marketing, more important than ever, and it's something I'll be paying more attention to now that I understand what's actually happening behind the "Islamic banking" label.
